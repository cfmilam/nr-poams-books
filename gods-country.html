Introduction

This is an account of the pure bare, innocent and unbidden experience of a child precipitated into a world divided between good and evil on the grandest scale. This was during the period of World War II. Others tell of such things as rationing, troop movements, with maps, reports and so on. This account is from a standpoint of sheer childish innocence. It is an account of what it was like to be a bare human consciousness with little or no knowledge of, or concern, for what was happening worldwide on a political scale. It is a tale of purely individual experience, of impressions received from real events, with no selection of what was 'relevant' or 'irrelevant' or even interesting to the course of the war. It cannot compete with the dramatic accounts of the terrifying experiences of people on the front line or in 'underground' movements such as those in Nazi occupied France – indeed, how could it, since, in Britain, children of that age were neither faced with those situations nor eligible for the forces.

However, such as it is, this account, for whatever they may be worth, are the thoughts of a wartime evacuee from the Welsh town of Swansea, which was assailed by the German Luftwaffe. It emphasises the formative influence which these events had on the personal and subsequent social life of this philosophically probing individual.

It was a fine, sunny day. Excitedly, I had wandered up from the farm in the valley, where I was staying as a wartime evacuee. Today was the day my parents and grandparents would be coming to visit me from Swansea, as they did on most weekends. On the way, they would pick up my sister, Ursula (four years my junior) from where she was evacuated near Llandeilo. After chatting with my hosts for a while we would drive off somewhere and have a picnic. Waiting, now, on the highest point of the hill, I could see all around me, the shimmering sunlit landscape, with its cloud shadows marching across the land. No sound but that of the skylarks in the clear blue above and the distant cawing of the crows on the hill opposite. It seemed to me I could see as far as forever. Way off in the distance were fields with their bountiful crops, and farms with leafy lanes leading invitingly towards them.

I could imagine their barns with atmospheric lofts full of scented hay and animal feed, with dusty sunbeams slanting through them, as in my memories of Aunty Sephora’s farm I used to be taken to visit in Carmarthenshire, during my earlier childhood, before the war.

I sat on the stand where the empty milk-churns stood, having been returned by the lorry from Llandeilo. With my thin bare arms resting cool against their silver surfaces, I dreamed – not the tired dreams of sleep but of profound wakefulness, of deep gut-wriggling excitement at contemplating how big the world was and what would be my future in it. I had always been interested in nature and in organising bits of it to do my bidding, like building dams in streams and diverting the water into places where it might otherwise never go. It had driven wheels for me and done other things that, when I grew up, I might implement on a much larger scale. The wind would work for me – I was fascinated with windmills. I felt sure that some day I would be someone to do things that mattered. Here I was, a small boy, to be sure, but a giant from where I stood right now. I would build a grown-up version of my Hornby railway, meandering through the grassy hills and tree-lined cuttings. I would find out in depth how engines worked,

View from the high point on the hill, now mostly obscured by nearly seventy years growth of vegetation.

what made things grow, what made it all so scented and beautiful, what it was that held me to the earth’s surface wherever I stood, sat or walked. I often lay on my back and throw a ball into the air from that position, marvelling at how it got just so far away, and then came back at me, as if by an elastic band or a magnet.

Magnets also fascinated me, as did spinning-tops and gyroscopes. One day I would find out what made those things behave in the strange ways they did. Particularly, I would find out how it was that I could see as far as I could. What was it that made that distance what it was; why light was so different from the dark; why it was coloured, why it formed beams and cast shadows. I would find out why I was here, why I was not born a sheep or a worm. With my senses filled to overflowing with all the sights, scents and sounds of that sylvan scene, all these ideas flowed through me like a flood. I was so thrilled by it all that it almost hurt.

I had always been enthralled by the phenomenon of distance. Distance intrigued me. And what intrigued me not least was that one had the means to change things at a distance. At home in my grandmother’s house, I would lay out the rails of my toy train from one room to another and set my engines and trucks to run on it, knowing that they continued on invisibly, under the coat-stands along the hall, until they reached my sister in the next room. She would turn the train around and it would come back to me with the trucks carrying the farm animals, formed out of lead, that we used to play with – dangerous toys, as regarded now, but of no such concern to anyone at that time. Then, in response to my sister’s notes in the trucks – for the market, as those notes had instructed – I would turn the train around and send it back with all sorts of things, including my battered lead soldiers with their many-times decapitated heads stuck back on with matchsticks, not much of a deal, she would undoubtedly have thought.

What fascinated me most about this was that the train continued dutifully to obey my intention, when it was beyond my reach and out of sight – especially in the tunnels I constructed out of rugs and furniture. The rails were the conduits of my purposes. The same applied to what my grandmother called my ‘cords and cottons’ that, to her annoyance, I often strung around the house and, on fine days, from my bedroom window down to the back gate. These had pulley-wheels

attached to carry my Meccano-constructed air-trolleys and other devices from place to place.

As I got older, these conduits would become more and more sophisticated, graduating from trains on rails to water in pipes, electric currents along wires and, eventually, radio. This fascinated me most of all, to do things right here which affected things over there. With some friends, I experimented with this principle, studying how radios and aerials worked. From bits of junk procured from scrap yards I built radio sets with which, under starry skies, on a bare hill above Manselton some friend and I would operate the 'cat's whisker' crystal until, in the headphones, we located, by sheer chance, some far-off station and listened to the undulating wash of its voices and music, which came and went like something drifting in and out with some ethereal surf.

All this, however, was to come later. For now, as I waited, I continued to stare in wonder at the bright, softly hissing distance of the world around me, punctuated as it was by the sharply pinpointed and comforting sounds of life nearby. And then, into this ensemble there gradually infiltrated the sound of a vehicle coming up the hill. I couldn't see it at first because it was hidden by the brow. But that sound was as familiar to me as my own heartbeat. I knew that it was our old 'Standard Nine'. I had been with my father when he bought it from Barlow's Garage, in Cockett, Swansea – second-hand, of course, since although well-enough provided, we were not exactly rich. My father, though, was an excellent mechanic. His father-in-law, my Grandfather Jones, had recently retired from his job as the boss of the bar-mill in Cwmfelin steel works, in Swansea. My father, meanwhile, was a telephone lineman in Swansea, in a reserved occupation, keeping the telephone communications going during the air-raids. He and my grandfather Jones always got on very well, my father with his spanners repairing the car and my grandfather with his small forge and anvil making any bits and bobs that might be needed for all sorts of things around the house. The biscuit-tin panniers my grandfather had made to fit the running board of an earlier car – a splendid Citroen, which had to be scrapped because there were no parts for it – would, I knew, be full of delicious sandwiches, with bread cut so thin in the way that only my Grandmother Jones could manage.

And now, here they were, coming into view, my sister waving to me excitedly, anticipating the fun we would have at the farm and on the picnic. Another thing that intrigued me about distance was how familiar people and great solid things like cars and trains could just disappear into it, becoming smaller and smaller, like microbes as they got farther and farther until they were swallowed up, and then reappearing later as they were now.

Then they were there, right alongside me. There stood the Standard Nine, with the smell of hot engine oil shimmering around the bonnet, and the scent of sunshine on metal bodywork and fabric. Even the tang of the exhaust blended sweetly with the scents of fields and hedgerows that had been my sole companions while I waited. The ensemble of my exhilaration had swelled into an orchestra, and now the orchestra reached a crescendo.

After driving us down the track to visit my hosts, my father would drive us to somewhere like Paxton’s Folly, which was where we often went when they came to see us. The tower overlooked the River Towy that meandered through the valley towards Carmarthen, stretching far

away westward of the same valley that was overlooked from where I had stood on the hill. Was it my father or my grandfather who, viewing this setting on an earlier occasion, I had heard breathe to himself: 'This is God's country!'?

My father, Don Pope, was part English, but my mother and both her parents were Welsh, her second name being – almost stereotypically Welsh – Blodwen Jones. And Welsh was the first language of my grandparents. But they didn't encourage my sister and me to speak the language, because it was not fashionable in the industrial town of Swansea, and there was nothing, then, of the movement towards preserving the language that there is now. They believed, caringly enough for us children, that it would disadvantage us educationally in the more or less anglicised town in which they were now settled. My grandfather had moved to Swansea from his home in the small farming village of Llanybri, to find work in the town and my grandmother had come from St Clears. Being both of them Carmarthenshire born and bred, they spoke English very much in the Welsh idiom. The only time they spoke fluent Welsh was when they quarrelled – which, happily, was not often.

My grandfather, Tom Jones, was a stern-looking, moustachioed man, who could have been cast in a cowboy film just as he was without makeup, as a steely-eyed Western gunslinger. His craggy countenance, though, belied his temperament, which was extremely kindly – as I remember it, at any rate. He was also devoutly religious, he and his wife, Isabella (née Thomas) being ardent chapel-goers. He called her Bella – or, on most occasions, Merch (Woman). They often entertained people from the Gors Mission in the parlour of their house in Cwmdu, where we all lived, they, my father, mother, sister and me – all six of us in an end-of-terrace house, named 'Cilrhedyn', at 28 Megan Street. I well remember those meetings between men in white collars, in the front room filled with pipe and cigar smoke. With its towering dark mahogany polished furniture, it was a holy-of-holies which I entered only fearfully and briefly. From that quarter, the 'fear of God' was instilled into me quite literally.

My father, though, was not of that – or any other – religious persuasion. He was of an entirely free-minded and tolerant, philosophical disposition, a man who could enjoy his own counsel and company as

much as that of his many friends. These friends were no doubt much appreciative of his ready wit and sense of humour which, in his responses to situations, ‘collapsed’ me on many occasions. His way of dealing with detractors was, to me, enviable. I often wished I had inherited his ready, Bernard-Shaw-like wit in dealing with people. It was a wit that I, most regrettably, could never match. I was more naturally inclined towards my mother’s way of dealing directly with sniping innuendos and other means of direct and naked aggression. In other words, like my sister and our mother, I was quick tempered and precipitate. (My wife sought long and hard to train me out of this but never entirely succeeded.)

My father’s leisure interests, whenever life allowed him to follow them, lay very much in things scientific and philosophical, being an amateur astronomer – later to become an official of the Swansea Astronomical Society. He was as tall and spare as my mother was dumpy, bosomy and comfy. He was as liberally minded and rational as she was impulsive and emotional. He was also highly volatile, with a towering temper when aroused, and some of the rows they had – mostly over her extreme irrationality in matters related to the allocation of his wages for this or that purpose, or the educational prospects of us children, were memorable to say the least. My mother had all sorts of plans mapped out for me in that direction. I felt that most of all she would like me to have become a pregethwr (preacher). This would not have suited me at all. On one occasion, catching me straining at a chest-expander that my father had bought for me, she asked me – in typically Welsh idiom: ‘What are you killing yourself like that for?’ ‘I’m trying to make myself strong, Mumma,’ I replied. ‘For Heaven’s sake,’ she said, crossly, ‘do you want to end up with strong old arms like your father!’ I dared not tell her what I really thought, which was yes, that was exactly what I wanted.

Strong and macho was not something my mother admired in men. Gentility was what she much preferred. My father was, basically, all of that, although his father, Jim Pope, was only a self-employed local cobbler, who was nevertheless of that same manly ilk. My father’s family were not so Welsh as my maternal grandparents, but they were of a more educated lineage, mostly on the side of his mother, Edith Pope (née Pegler) who had been trained and educated as a teacher.

There was some talk that my paternal grandfather, Jim Pope, was somehow related, on his mother’s side, to Christopher Wren. Edith’s father, my great grandfather, Alfred Pegler, who had been a ‘compositor’ for a printing firm was, I was told, something of a poet and playwright, an intellectual, with all the eccentricity and absent-mindedness that goes with it. As for my father, gentle – indeed, genteel – as he usually was would sometimes, and mostly at my instigation, turn into what seemed to me a tyrannosaurus rex. I think, though, that he had an amazing amount of patience with me, perhaps far more than I deserved. In all, he was a man’s man through and through, far from being like the Come-to-Jesus-collared, clean-cut, Bible-thumping clergymen who had courted my mother when she was a girl. Indeed, I often wondered how it was that she could ever have chosen my father above all the many suitors who had surrounded her in that chapel-going fraternity.

If I have given the impression that my father was a violent tempered man, that is not strictly true. As a youth, his own father, a large and imposing man, had controlled him ‘with a strap’ as they used to say. Don was a well-renowned swimmer but with no trophies or anything to record it. He had no pretensions of that clubbable sort. I heard from others – including my wife’s father who, as a younger boy, knew my father’s reputation – of how, in ‘The Bounds’ (the ‘out-of-bounds’ reservoir of the Cwmbwrla steelworks, in which there had been many accidental drownings), my father was the only one who had the temerity to dive deep down the underwater remains of a chimney-stack and come out through a narrow hole in the base.

In a temper, my father would become something of a hylozoist. I well remember an incident when I was playing outside my grandfather’s shed in which my father kept his motor-bike and sidecar. I could hear him humming away contentedly as he tinkered with the engine of his motorbike – he had a very deep voice and the humming was more like a subterranean rumble than a tune. Then he tried to kick-start the engine, but it stubbornly refused to run. After a number of attempts there was a pregnant silence. Then my father said – to the motorbike apparently, because there was no-one else present – ‘Look, I’m bloody-well warning you . . .’ and the rest was lost amidst a simian kind of growling.

Mostly, however, I roamed contentedly on my own. One day, wandering in this way across fields, fences and streams, I came to a road and found myself in a place which looked somewhat familiar. It was where my sister had been dropped from the bus that had delivered us evacuees to our foster homes. I remembered how delightedly the lady had greeted Ursula. What a nice lady, I thought, happy that my sister had found a comfortable billet. 'I only wish,' the lady had proclaimed to the bus driver, 'that I could have had the brother as well!' (Unfortunately, as it would turn out, Ursula's experience of this evacuation would not be nearly so pleasant as mine.)

I had been the last one to be dropped off that day. It was long after the others had been delivered. This was in a single-decker local bus which, when climbing the steep hills, had a gear-whine like the mournful 'lost chord' of a humming top. By then it was early evening, as I recall, and it seemed to me that the driver was impatient to finish and get home. The hills were now covered by a soaking mist. I was dropped, with my small case, at the top of a hill near a gate, with the bus-driver's instruction to 'follow the track until you get to the farm'.

It was, however, in the wrong place, some distance, as it turned out, from the farm. The visibility, by now, had shut down to just yards, and I had to trek mostly through long grass, as wet as grass can get without being actually under water. I can't remember being scared, just bewildered and bemused, as if in a dream.

Somehow, I reached the farm. It had loomed out of the mist like some sort of stone-grey castle. Wondering which was the way into it, eventually I found what I assumed to be the front door. In the state of mind I was then in, I was surprised it didn't have a moat and a drawbridge. I felt extremely small.

I must have knocked on the door (my recall of what happened after this is no longer coherent, so confused were my emotions at the time). However, that is another story. Getting back to the place I had happened upon whilst wandering around the countryside, the house I was now looking at was the one at which Ursula had been delivered on the day of our evacuation. It stood in its own in grounds which were quite large compared to the gardens of the houses in Swansea. In an orchard I spotted Ursula, playing in the sunshine, as much on her own as I had been. She was delighted to see me, and her shouts brought the lady of

the house into the garden to see what was going on. I can’t remember how – so gauche and inarticulate was I as a lad – but somehow I prevailed on the lady of the house to let Ursula come and wander the countryside with me. I took her back to the farm with me, where she was shown, among other interesting things, the caged ferret – which she insisted on calling a ‘newt’. There was also a buck rabbit which, almost at its last gasp, I had rescued from a snare and which I had put in an empty barrel with a whole cabbage, hoping it would recover, which I feared it wouldn’t. Ursula was watching as I removed the lid of the barrel. She laughed in delight, not only at the animal’s escape as also how startled her big and brave brother was as he staggered backwards at the sudden emergence of a very large and vigorous, well-recovered rabbit which leaped up in the air and literally hit the ground running. It was like old times. Ursula, just four years younger, had always been a tomboy and was, in some ways, more like a younger

brother to me than a sister. For the rest of the day we had a whale of a time, so interested was she in my showing her around the farm with its animals, the cowshed, the dairy, the gorgeously coloured strutting cockerel, the barns, pig-sties, the geese and the rest.

Eventually, I walked her back to where she was staying. It was by now quite late afternoon and getting cooler. As we approached the house, Ursula got quieter. The place now seemed somewhat ominous, despite the sunshine still around it. Accompanying Ursula, I was invited in and stayed with her there for a short while before I left. The interior seemed dark and dismal. In the corner was an old man, seated in what seemed to be a rocking chair. However, the figure was without movement, and silent as if it were a mummy. The sense of ominousness I had felt previously was now heightened.

While I was there, Ursula gave vent to some of her natural boisterousness, which did not seem to be well appreciated by her host, and as I took leave of them, I saw her receive a hard smack by the woman for what seemed to me no reason at all. This disturbed me all the way back to the farm. The bad feeling stayed with me so much so that when my family came to visit the next weekend, I told them of the incident. I don't know what transpired at the house where Ursula was staying, but my parents took Ursula away with them, back to Swansea. Although there was no such problem in my case – I had been extremely well treated and cared for on the farm – Ursula's return to Swansea made me homesick and the next time they visited I was taken away, too, with, as I gleaned, the profoundest apologies and gratitude from my parents to my hosts and much sympathy and understanding from them.

Prior to that, my wanderings led to my coming across Melvyn (Mel) Charles, the brother of the legendary footballer, John, with both of whom I had been in the same childhood street-gang in Cwmdu and in the same school in Cwmbwrla. I spotted Melvyn kicking a ball against an outhouse wall. Our boisterous greetings caused Melvyn's host, a very nice-seeming comely lady to come out and see what was happening. After I had enjoyed a kickabout with Melvyn, this lady called us in for a cake and a cup of tea. What a nice billet Mel had obtained, I thought. But then, as I was preparing to leave, this nice lady turned to me and staggered me by saying, in Mel's presence, 'I

wish I'd been given a nice white lad like you instead of a "blackie" like this one.' The Charles boys' mother, a lovely hardworking, well-respected and kindly woman was I later learned, 'coloured'. This had not in the least concerned my family or any others in the district of Cwmdu. Indeed, one of these 'coloured' gentleman was a well-known local hero who had saved a boy from drowning by diving into the filthy water of the dreaded 'Bounds'. It was only after I and my family moved to England that we became aware of something called a 'colour bar'. In the South Wales seaport town of Swansea, where there were so many different racial strains, so far as I knew no such thing was ever discussed. (It would surely have been to this racist woman's shame to realise that the 'blackie' she was hosting, who was to become a redoubtable football player in his own right, was the brother of the man who would become, arguably, the most gifted sportsman in the history of international football!)

Anyway, after the incident with Ursula's host, somehow or other – I can't recall the details – I was taken back to Swansea in the train. This was by my mother and father, and Ursula was with us. As we approached Swansea, it got dark, with the stygian darkness of the 'black-out'. The train was stopped, time and again, for long periods. This was due to what we were told were air-raid warnings in the town. The night had a crackling kind of stillness, with the sort of conditioned tension that follows the sound of an air-raid siren. The world seemed to hold its breath. The train moved forward only in spurts. Eventually we reached the station, only to find that there were no buses running from there. We were told we would have to walk up from Victoria Station to Christina Street, in the centre of the town if we wanted to catch a bus to Cwmdu, where we lived.

When we got to Christina Street, there stood the buses, with their slitted headlights and sidelights. We boarded the one marked 'Cwmbwrla', only to find that it would be going nowhere while the raid was on. So we got out and started to walk the rest of the long way home.

And then, on the way towards Cwmbwrla, the raid opened up and we had to duck into a concrete air-raid shelter – in Tontine Street, I think it was. Inside the shelter, people sat huddled, singing. Then, as the eldritch screams of bombs were followed by the shuddering thumps and thuds of their explosions, interspersed with the ear-

slamming bangs of the anti-aircraft batteries and the crackle of flak, it was amazing to me with what suddenness the bawdy singing and capering turned into the most devoutly religious kneeling, chanting and praying.

In the lulls between the waves of action, my father walked us further towards our destination just outside the town. We had to duck into other shelters on the way, and I was intrigued that the same songs were being desperately bawled out in practically all those different places. And of course, I was frightened, for sure, but mostly I was puzzled. Why were these Germans bombing us? What had we done to them? I failed to understand. It was politics, of course, but that meant little to me at the time.

What is politics, anyway, I later reflected, but the art of living in an irrational society. In a rational world, what need would there be for politics? This made me an idealist, of course. I had somehow imagined, in my boyhood innocence, that the truth and reality of things could be uncovered by what I later came to identify as logical reason. (Indeed, in later life I was to become a teacher of Logic and Scientific Method.) But from where had I obtained this notion of rationality? I must have imbibed it from my father. I somehow imagined that everyone was as liberal and rational as he was. It was plain to me that without logicality there could be no honesty, no real clarity of thought or even of opinion. People without logic could think whatever they liked and get away with it. Without logic, what else could 'thinking' be but a muddy swirl of impressions and emotions? Victims of their own whims and fancies, illogical people could fool anyone, not least themselves. I felt I would prefer to live in a rational world, even if there was to be no-one else in it but myself – as it had seemed from my height on the hill above Golden Grove. An idealist, yes, but better be an idealist with his feet planted firmly in the air than a materialist with his head buried firmly in the ground.

There would be other raids, of course, some much worse than the one into which we had been precipitated by the train from Llandeilo. There would be one in particular in which I was just sure I was going to die. A stick of bombs fell right across us at Megan Street, destroying houses in streets to either side. 'Strange,' I mused, resignedly, as the bombs screamed down on us, 'that in the morning I won't be here

and others will be left to read about it in the paper! Where will I be then?' I wondered, philosophically, as the air-raid shelter rattled, heaved and shook.

Those bombs, to say the least, had landed far too close for comfort. My future wife's grandparents, a few streets away, were bombed out that night, and her grandfather had to be rescued after being buried, bent double, under a pile of debris. Her uncle had an incendiary bomb blow up in his face, when he was called to deal with it in the lane behind the house opposite where I lived. From these injuries he soon died. The house just opposite to where my Grandparents Pope lived, in Middle Road, was obliterated, and in Vicarage Lane, just behind our house, two bombs dropped, one of them demolishing a very nice old cottage.

After that, Ursula and I were evacuated once again. This was to another idyllic Carmarthenshire spot near Talley, outside Llandeilo. The house my mother had now rented was what had once been a hostelry, a half-way house – indeed, that was its postal address: 'Half-Way, Talley'. It had a dairy at the back, with a trout-stream channelled through the floor, through the south end of which the sun slanted through the clear water to dapple the bed of the stream with the fish waving in the flow trying to look like weeds.

Half Way, Talley, 2008.

When we returned to Swansea, after a lull in the bombing, the raids started again and once more we were caught in the midst of it. This was in a district of Swansea called Brynmill, near Swansea Bay, in another house my parents had rented. In our stay there, there was more bombing and just before we arrived a three-storey house had been destroyed, on the corner opposite. After one raid I found an unexploded incendiary bomb, the fins of which were sticking out from a near-by bowling green. I pulled it up and took it home to polish it and show it proudly to mother and my school friends. 'Very nice', said my mother and gave me some 'Glitto' to make it more shiny. I cherished it for some hours – until my father came home and saw it, but that is another story, to which I shall return later. We were near some special guns on the promenade facing Swansea Bay, and during a raid the bangs they made were so loud as to be viscerally disturbing, especially at night.

Eventually, we left Brynmill to return to my grandparents' house in Megan Street, Cwmdu, where there were more raids again – the bombers seemed to be following us! Returning from a friend's house one night, I was caught in one of the raids and stood on a wall to watch, in utter fascination, a nearby paper factory in Gors Road, Cwmbwrla, in the valley just across the way, near the railway track, set ablaze by incendiary bombs of the same kind that I had dug up and taken home. The fire was so fierce that even at that distance I felt the heat of it on my face. Then suddenly, once again, I was caught by the scruff and shaken by my father, who had been out looking for me. 'It's alright, Daddy,' I stuttered, 'I had my helmet on!' This was a Woolworth's toy helmet that my friend and I had been playing with at his house. I was testing the helmet by wearing it and inviting him to bang spoons on it, proudly declaring: 'No, that didn't hurt' when it definitely did. In spite of my 'hang-ups' about killing, I had excelled in my training and physical pursuits in general. I was chosen as 'Senior Man' of my group. I also excelled in the estimation of the corporals and sergeants, and was eventually selected as the top recruit of my intake, being the one 'talked to' by the AOC (Air Officer Commanding) on Pass-out Parade. I was also chosen as a temporary conscript into the RAF Special Police (the 'SPs') during an outbreak of riots among cadets at Cranwell, and was used as a demonstration model during training for a public gymnastic display. At the camp to which I was sent after training I organised and ran a body-building club. All this was of great satisfaction to my ego and general self-regard. Yet, throughout it all I

remained split-minded about it. My alter ego kept nagging me about the vanity and ineffectuality of it all. Something inside nagged at me that none of this really mattered, that it was not the sort of life I had anticipated as a lad in those halcyon days at Golden Grove. What, I wondered, was the ideal end-aim of life to be, a heaven or a Valhalla? On times I would wander away on my own to think about it – to ‘sort myself out’, as I told myself. At other times, I was an extravert to the point of lunacy, and I was aware that some of my extremest and stupidest exploits had, albeit unintentionally, become legendary.

I had scarcely emerged from boyhood when I met the girl I would marry and who would become everything to me. She would be the mother of my children and the grandmother of theirs in their turn. This relationship put a curb on my natural excesses and encouraged what only she had seen as the best in me. She was the only one my grandmother had liked among the gang of girls with whom my friends and I consorted during our early years of puberty. And my grandmother’s intuitions had proved correct, although, sadly, she never lived to see it. Mary and I were complete opposites – different animals in many ways. We were, I felt, the complete Yin and Yang, as the Chinese see it, of the relation of the sexes. She was a natural rule-follower and scholar and, when I met her, was being groomed for an academic career at either Oxford or Cambridge. But her career in that respect had been blighted from the start of her meeting with me, to the chagrin of her parents and teachers who did all they could to break us apart – especially her father, who disliked me intensely. However, from her I eventually imbibed the arts and literary education I had systematically escaped in my wanderings away from school. Not only did she educate me, selflessly and stealthily as only a woman can, but also she educated the whole family and was the last one of us to graduate, which she did in her fifties.

Unlike Mary, I never took to formal Education. Schooling had bored me to near-catatonia. My mind was always elsewhere, away from stuffy old classrooms with their buff-coloured walls, away from books, tests and exams, roaming wraith-like outside in the country. ‘Mitching’ from school as often as I could, I would wander around the docks and scrap-yards, picking up interesting bits and bobs, taking them home to my workshop (a modified Anderson air-raid shelter) for

examination as to possible use, as if each visit had been a plundering of Aladdin’s Cave. Also, some of my earliest memories were of messing around with my Meccano set and the interesting things in my Grandfather Jones’ shed, not least the huge anvils, vices and hammers he so often used to forge bits of metal. He had shown me how to make ‘slaked lime’ from limestone beach pebbles, heated in the fire and then dropped into a bucket of water. This he then used for painting walls and, mixed with ashes, turned into black mortar for bricklaying and stone-setting. He also taught me, during our family excursions, how to make whips out of reeds and whistles out of tree-bark; to find streams for the gathering of water-cress. He also showed me, on the estuary at Llansteffan, how to catch flatfish with a three-pronged fork he had forged in his shed. Although I spotted many of them stirring up clouds of sand, I never caught a single fish by this method and, in my excitement, barely missed spiking my own feet with it.

Sometimes, mostly when ill in bed, recovering from some illness or other, I would pick through the weighty, sweet-scented, beautifully illustrated encyclopaedias that my parents had bought for us. These I would scan through avidly, even if only reading the legends accompanying the pictures, while my grandmother brought me plates of my beloved salt-buttered thin-cut bread. From all this I gained a love of science that never left me. I read about people like Columbus and Copernicus, Galileo and Newton, as well as many others who, each in his day, had so spectacularly changed the course of men’s thinking away from channels of witchcraft and superstition into the paths of true rational science. I learned about how these mental giants had had to fight the Establishment, so bravely and often in fear of their very lives, to rescue truth from the stranglehold of sheer stupidity.

It was with all this love of science and sweet reason already infused into me that I had sat amongst those cool milk-churns on their stand in the heart of ‘God’s country’. I hadn’t even the vaguest notion, at that time, of where this love of science would lead me or the heartache it would eventually involve.

My reading taught me that there had been times in our history when, in order to keep science squared with nature, it had been necessary for it to make radical and indeed revolutionary changes against all the resistance convention could muster. It made me wonder, could

any such revolution ever happen again? I tried to think how it would have been, when everyone thought the earth was flat to have discovered that it is round. I felt that watching ships disappear over the horizon, I would surely have worked that out for myself, by plain commonsense. But then I imagined reporting this to someone, only to be told, 'That's ridiculous! If the earth were round, then all the oceans would flow around it and fall off underneath!'

This put me in mind of Copernicus who, when the 'going rate' among the populace at large was to think of the Earth as the God-created centre of the universe, had discovered that the Earth, like all the other planets, circled the sun. I read that he dared not publish this discovery for fear of religious persecution. I remembered also reading about Giordano Bruno who, supporting the Copernican view, taught what everyone now knows, that our earth and all the various planets and satellites have surfaces like our earth and are no more than just rocks, like our own planet, all orbiting the sun. I read that for his trouble, this got Bruno, together with his books, burned at the stake. Then there was Galileo who was threatened with torture for supporting the Copernican view. And when, with his primitive telescope, he projected an image of the sun onto a screen to reveal what we now know as sunspots, he was accused of 'trafficking with the Devil' in devising and employing of instruments suggesting that God had created a 'flawed luminary'. What has absolutely staggered me, only recently, was that in view of how modern cosmology and technology supported him, Galileo was 'forgiven' by the Catholic Church for what they charged him at the time as 'heresy'.

For a long time, I firmly believed that those days of social stupidity were over, and that science had now reached its final, finished form. I felt satisfied that it was now both theoretically and practically replete, and I pursued my interests in it avidly. But then, as my probings continued, I gradually came to realise that something was seriously amiss. My earlier 'love of science' was now being tested to destruction. I was forcefully reminded of the sorts of things that had happened to those earlier questioners, such as Copernicus, Galileo and the others. How, I wondered, would I have felt then? Much the same as now, I imagined. Those things had definitely happened and now it seemed to me that they were about to happen again.

What disturbed me was that the science I had so much loved was now, in the name of Modern Physics, starting to part company from logical reason. Moreover, its cosmology was now more absurd than anything I’d read about previously – more absurd even than that of the Hindus, who believed that the world stood on the backs of elephants standing on a huge turtle swimming in an infinite flat sea. Science, it seemed to me, had rescued natural philosophy from the clutches of Religion only to plunge it into a new form of Religion, ruled by a new and incipient kind of priesthood. Priests! I thought. What sorts of creatures were these? I was reminded of what history tells us of the priests who persuaded people to burn their children to propitiate what they imagined to be ‘The Gods’. Whatever was the matter with us? We seemed, as a species, to be fundamentally flawed. Whatever was it, I wondered, that made us prone to such complete and utter nonsense.

The more I thought about these departures of science from commonsense reason, the more ridiculous they became. There were, nowadays as in the past, so many things that people accepted without question as ‘scientific’ but which even the slightest attention to logic revealed were just plain claptrap. For instance, returning to my earlier thoughts about light and distance with my adult mind, I now posed a series of fundamental questions about the received opinions of the experts on the subject of light. These told me that light travelled invisibly – that is, what else but darkly – as ‘waves in a vacuum’. In a vacuum? But what was there to ‘wave’ in a vacuum? And in a vacuum, what was there against which to measure the speed of anything, constant or otherwise? And what about the way things affected one another at a distance? How did the moon and sun manage to raise the tides on earth? Again I was told that this was due to an inscrutable ‘force’ acting ‘in the vacuum’. In the vacuum, I was told, all bodies wanted to travel in straight lines but were prevented from doing so by those invisible ‘forces’ by which all bodies magically attract one another across the void. So, I asked myself, when do bodies travel in straight lines in the way we were told? The answer was never, because these invisible ‘forces’ are always present to hide the straight-line motion we would otherwise observe. But what a con! To me it was pure nonsense to attribute to a ‘vacuum’ such a holding strength that could not be matched by even the thickest bunches of steel cables!

The logic of it was like that of the joke about the man who claimed payment for keeping the tigers down in Hyde Park. 'But there are no tigers in Hyde Park,' he was told. 'Which only shows what a good job I'm doing,' he replied.

As I would advance in my search for natural truth, the more of these self-inflicted cons I would eventually uncover and the more this would enable me to empathise with those others I had read about who had sought, against all the threats of torture and so on, to steer science on an honest, socially disinterested, logical course. There was, it seemed, simply no joined-up logical thinking in modern science. It had lost all philosophical integrity and become completely incoherent, although, like the curate's egg, it was 'good in parts'. And whenever I talked or wrote about this, all I got was sheer negativity. A typical reaction was from a science scholar my brother-in-law, John Edwards, had known at university. John had asked this friend of his to comment on a piece of my work. 'Not me!' his friend wrote to him, 'I don't wish to be listed as one of the names in your brother-in-law's "Famous People I have Been Rude To" book.' This was surely the most deeply revealing statement about the ego of that commentator himself. It was on a par with what some scientist was reported to have said. 'Science is going to the dogs. Galileo is dead, Newton is dead, Einstein is dead, and I'm not feeling too well, myself.'

In a way, these sorts of reactions to my probings – and there were many of these self-revealing reactions – were reassuring. They suggested to me that, at least, I was doing something significant enough to create a back-pressure of classic proportions. But what surprised and distressed me most was the sheer nastiness that this progress generated among my close friends and associates – and even, strange to relate, members of my family. (My father was no longer around, of course. He had died of heart-disease just days before his fiftieth birthday.) And what seemed most paradoxical was that the people who knew least about orthodox science were often the most vigorous in defending it. They were passively accepting a Science they did not understand, through nothing more than awe of its reputation and authority.

Summary

What is more real? Is it the chronicled history of events or one's own actual experience of those events as they happened? And what, indeed, is history without those direct experiences? Those events in which I was involved, such as the raids, the evacuation, the sentiments, feelings and fears they evoked, the way they directed this one person's life, what can these matter in an entirely objective, historical account? Nothing? No, perhaps everything, since it is the actions of individuals, forged by their personal experiences that make history. History can be nothing without them.

I have always been aware of being such an individual. I cannot think, feel or perceive as a crowd. I have no doubt that my life, thought and intention were shaped by my experiences both in the madness of the wartime blitzes and in the comparative peace of my sojourn in 'God's Country', which I might never have experienced but for the war. I am grateful for the circumstance that when I went to school at Talley there were no evacuees there with me for companionship, as there had been at Golden Grove, so no consideration was given to the

fact that I didn't speak Welsh, as all the other pupils did. This threw me on my own devices for learning about things, when, unbeknown to anyone, I went walkabout, exploring the beckoning countryside. This set the pattern for my future encounters with formal Education, from secondary school right up to university and beyond, any influence of which I accepted on my own terms, with logical criticality and rational scepticism.

We are, all of us, children of our times, and I am eternally thankful that, for all the difficulties and dangers of my childhood during the war, my time was then and not now.
